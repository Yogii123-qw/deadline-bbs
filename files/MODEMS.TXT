MODEMS, BAUD, AND THE ART OF THE BUSY SIGNAL
=============================================

WHAT "BAUD" MEANT
-----------------
Early callers connected at 300 baud: about 30 characters per
second. You could watch the text arrive. By 1994, a 14.4k modem
moved roughly 1,440 characters per second, and a 28.8k modem
was the dream machine. A 1MB file at 2400 baud took about an
hour. At 14.4k, about ten minutes. People planned downloads
around phone bills.

THE HANDSHAKE
-------------
Dialing a BBS sounded like two robots arguing: dial tone, rings,
then the screech of negotiation as the modems agreed on a speed
and an error-correction protocol. Veterans could tell the connect
speed by ear. A 14.4 handshake sounded different from a 2400 one,
and everyone had opinions about which was prettier.

AT COMMANDS (THE ONES EVERYONE KNEW)
-------------------------------------
AT          Say hello to the modem. It answers OK.
ATDT5551212 Dial the number (tone dialing).
ATH         Hang up.
ATZ         Reset the modem to defaults.
ATS0=1      Answer on the first ring (sysop mode).

WHY IT MATTERED
---------------
One phone line meant one caller. If someone was online, you got
a busy signal and tried again. Scarcity made every call feel
like an event, and made sysops the most patient people alive.

DeadLine BBS keeps the rituals and drops the busy signal: the
telnet port answers every caller, and the web terminal answers
the rest. The patience is still yours to bring.
