A SHORT HISTORY OF THE BULLETIN BOARD SYSTEM
==========================================

1978: THE FIRST CALL
The first public dial-up BBS, CBBS, went online on 16 February
1978 in Chicago, built by Ward Christensen and Randy Suess.
One computer, one modem, one caller at a time.

1980s: THE SCENE GROWS
Boards spread wherever phone lines reached. FidoNet (1984) let
boards exchange mail overnight, when long-distance rates dropped.
ANSI art turned 80x25 text screens into galleries. Door games
like TradeWars and Legend of the Red Dragon gave callers a
reason to dial in every single day.

1994: THE PEAK
InfoWorld estimated about 60,000 BBSes serving 17 million users
in the United States alone. For most people, a BBS was the first
online community they ever belonged to.

1995: THE CRASH
Cheap dial-up internet access and the Mosaic web browser caused
what historians describe as a rapid crash in the BBS market
starting in late 1994, and the sudden obsolescence of bulletin
board technology in 1995. The phone lines went quiet within
a couple of years.

TODAY: THE REFUSAL
The BBS never fully died. The Telnet BBS Guide listed about
1,046 boards as of June 2026, reachable over telnet instead of
modems. DeadLine BBS joins them: the same rituals, the same
patience, running on a modern stack.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Bulletin Board System"; InfoWorld estimate
as cited there; Telnet BBS Guide. Dates and figures as published.
